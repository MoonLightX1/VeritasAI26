Under the hood

How the forecast works and how we tested it. The code blocks were all run for real and the grey box under each one is what it printed.

1. How the forecast works

It all lives in backend/services/predict.py. When you hit Predict on a stock page, the server does this in the background:

  1. Get the price history from Yahoo Finance.
  2. Work out the trend and how jumpy the price is from the last 60 moves.
  3. Search the news. Seven searches run at once, repeats get dropped, and we keep the newest 45 headlines.
  4. Score every headline from -1 (bad) to +1 (good) using a keyword list.
  5. Context rules fix the dumb mistakes. Shows and movies get ignored, and executive scandals get pushed negative.
  6. Mix the scores into one mood number. Newer headlines count more.
  7. Draw the forecast line and the shaded range.

2. The AI part

The AI is a small LoRA adapter on top of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, so we didn't have to retrain the whole model. It runs from a folder on the same computer, so there are no API keys and nothing gets sent to an outside AI company. The test results below are for the keyword scorer and the forecast math.

3. Scoring headlines

The keyword scorer has two word lists, one good and one bad. It counts the hits, flips a word if "not" is right before it, then squashes the total so one loud word can't max it out. P below is predict.py.

# three headlines, good then bad then boring
heads = [
    "Apple beats earnings expectations as iPhone sales climb",
    "Tesla recalls thousands of vehicles over safety defect",
    "Apple to hold annual developer conference in June",
]
for h in heads:
    print(round(P._lexicon_score(h), 2), h)
1.0 Apple beats earnings expectations as iPhone sales climb
-0.8 Tesla recalls thousands of vehicles over safety defect
0.0 Apple to hold annual developer conference in June
# "not" right before a word flips it
print(P._lexicon_score("Ford sales strong"))
print(P._lexicon_score("Ford sales not strong"))
0.8
-0.8

The weak spot is that a good word and a bad word in the same headline cancel out, so real bad news can score zero.

# "downgraded" is -1 and "growth" is +1
print(P._lexicon_score("Netflix downgraded by analysts on slowing growth"))
print(P._lexicon_score("Spotify shares drop as subscriber growth disappoints"))
0.0
0.0

Context rules

A scary word isn't always bad news. A show called "The Affair" is not a scandal. So quoted titles get stripped, movie and trailer headlines get ignored, and a personal scandal only counts if an executive or owner is in the headline.

def judge(title, score):
    words = set(re.findall(r"[a-z\-]+", strip_titles(title).lower()))
    if words & ENTERTAINMENT and not (words & BUSINESS):
        return 0.0, False, "About a show, film or trailer, not the company's business"
    if personal_scandal(title):
        return min(score, -0.85), True, "Executive scandal"
    return score, True, ""
# judge gives back (score, counts or not, note)
for t in ["Best Netflix shows to binge this weekend",
          "Alphabet CEO accused of misconduct, board opens probe",
          "Apple to hold annual developer conference in June"]:
    s = P._lexicon_score(t)
    print(P.judge(t, s), "|", t)
(0.8, True, '') | Best Netflix shows to binge this weekend
(-0.85, True, 'Executive scandal') | Alphabet CEO accused of misconduct, board opens probe
(0.0, True, '') | Apple to hold annual developer conference in June

4. One mood number

Every headline score gets a weight that drops as the headline gets older (about 37 percent after 4 days). A small pile of headlines gets pulled toward zero because we're less sure. Serious negative headlines get an extra push down so ten "stock rises" stories can't hide a scandal.

# same very positive headline, half a day old, more and more of them
for n in [1, 3, 10, 20]:
    items, scores = pile(n)
    print(n, "headlines ->", round(P.combine(items, scores, now=now), 3))
1 headlines -> 0.469
3 headlines -> 0.726

# ten great headlines and one executive scandal

0.489

One glowing headline only gets to 0.47, ten get to 0.90, and ten plus one scandal drops to 0.49. The 0.47 and the 4 day fade are numbers we picked, not something we proved.

5. The forecast line

The trend is the average move per day and the swing is how jumpy the price is. We cap the trend at 1.5 times the swing so it can't run wild. Most of the move happens early and then levels off, a price that ran too far gets pulled back toward its average, and the shaded range is 1.28 times the swing times the square root of the days ahead (an 80 percent range).

# 30 day forecast on a made up stock. lo and hi are the shaded range
f = P.forecast(pts, 0.0, 30)
print("start", round(pts[-1]["c"], 2))
for k in [0, 1, 9, 29]:
    print("day", k + 1, "lo", f[k]["lo"], "line", f[k]["c"], "hi", f[k]["hi"])
start 147.96
day 1 lo 146.74 line 149.47 hi 152.24
day 2 lo 148.21 line 152.12 hi 156.14
day 10 lo 145.85 line 154.59 hi 163.86
day 30 lo 138.5 line 153.2 hi 169.46

Same prices, different news moods:

for s in [-0.8, 0.0, 0.8]:
    f = P.forecast(pts, s, 30)
    print("news mood", s, "-> ends at", f[-1]["c"])
news mood -0.8 -> ends at 137.24
news mood 0.0 -> ends at 153.2
news mood 0.8 -> ends at 153.11

The last row isn't a typo. Strong news takes the place of the trend instead of adding to it, and this made up stock was already trending up faster than the news drift, so good news came out a hair lower.

6. Test: reading headlines

We wrote 63 headlines by hand in the style of real financial news: 20 good, 20 bad, 15 routine, and 8 that aren't about the business at all. We labeled each one ourselves and ran the keyword scorer and context rules on them.

# the 63 headline test
from headlines import H
names = {"positive": "good", "negative": "bad", "neutral": "neutral", "irrelevant": "irrelevant"}
right = 0
for t, truth in H:
    s, rel_ok, _ = P.judge(t, P._lexicon_score(t))
    lab = P.label_for(s) if rel_ok else "irrelevant"
    right += names[lab] == truth
print("with rules:", right, "of", len(H))
with rules: 55 of 63

55 of 63 right (87.3 percent). Always guessing the biggest group would only get 31.7 percent. With the context rules turned off it drops to 79.4 percent (50 of 63). With only 63 headlines the real accuracy could be anywhere from about 77 to 93 percent, so don't treat 87.3 as exact.

7. Test: the forecast on made up stocks

Each made up stock has 250 days of random daily moves (about 2 percent a day). We ask the real forecast function for 30 days ahead, make up what "actually" happens, and check how it did. 1,500 stocks per scenario. Using made up prices means we know exactly how the fake world works, but it also means this says nothing about real markets.

Both charts start from the same calm history. Left: the future is as calm as the past, and 23 of 30 days landed inside the range (77 percent, close to the 80 promised). Right: the future is 3 times as jumpy and 0 of 30 landed inside. These are single tries, not averages.

What it shows

  • When the fake world acts the way the code expects, the range does its job. 80.4 percent against 80 promised.
  • Across all days it comes up a little short (76 percent), because the jumpiness is guessed from only 60 past moves.
  • When volatility suddenly jumps, the range only catches about 42 percent while still claiming 80. It only knows the past.
  • Direction was right about half the time everywhere, which is a coin flip. In the fake world the news has no link to prices, so there was nothing to find. With 1,500 trials a coin flip wobbles by about 1.3 percent, and all five results are within that of 50.
  • Strong news that was pure noise made the average miss about 28 percent worse than saying "no change" (10.75 versus 8.40). That's the cost of tilting the line on news that doesn't matter.

9. Does it beat a baseline?

A baseline is the dumbest reasonable guess. If 87 out of 100 emails aren't spam, a "model" that always says "not spam" is already 87 percent right, so a score only means something next to its baseline. analysis-tests/baseline.py does this.

Headlines

Baseline 2: keywords only50 of 6379.4%68 to 88%

The model beats always guessing by about 56 points. The context rules add about 8 points over plain keywords, but the ranges overlap, so with 63 headlines we can't say for sure the rules help.

11. What went wrong

  • Words with two meanings fool the word lists ("fall" the season, for example).
  • A good word and a bad word in one headline cancel out, so real bad news can score neutral.
  • "better than expected" slips by because "better" isn't on the list.
  • Non-business headlines only get ignored if they contain one of the listed entertainment words.
  • "fined" isn't on the serious list, so a regulator fine doesn't get the extra push down.
  • If the AI's direction and number disagree, the code goes with the direction word.
  • The shaded range is way too narrow after a sudden jump in volatility.
  • Tilting the line on news that doesn't matter makes it worse than "no change".
  • Strong news replaces the trend instead of adding to it, so on a stock that's already rising fast, good news can lower the forecast a hair.

This is a hackathon project, not financial advice. The forecast was only tested on made up prices, so it's for learning and shouldn't be used to trade real money.

12. How we tested

  • Source: backend/services/predict.py, news.py, market.py and routers/stocks.py.
  • Tests are Python scripts that call the real functions from predict.py, run offline. The 63 headlines and the simulation settings are in the analysis-tests folder (headlines.py, run1 to run4). examples.py makes the code blocks on this page and baseline.py does the baseline comparison.
  • We wrote the headlines, the labels and the made up price worlds ourselves. None of it is real news or real prices.
  • If you rerun the simulations the numbers can shift a little with the random seed. The headline numbers should match exactly.
